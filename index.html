THE PROBLEM

Mountains of food waste

Homes, schools and restaurants throw out peels, scraps and leftovers every day. Most of it ends up in landfills.

Heat that just escapes

When microbes break down organic waste, the pile warms up. That heat leaks into the air and does nothing.

Our idea

Hold on to some of that heat, run it through a thermoelectric generator, and show a small electrical output.

OUR MODEL

EcoTherm is a small working demonstration of how heat from decomposing kitchen waste can be captured and turned into a little electrical energy. It has six main parts.

Plastic decomposition chamber

Holds the waste and keeps the heat inside while the microbes work.

Biodegradable kitchen waste

Fruit peels and vegetable scraps. This is the heat source, so no fuel is needed.

Metal heat-transfer element

Metal conducts heat well, so it carries warmth from the waste to the TEG.

TEG module

The thermoelectric generator. It turns the temperature difference between its two faces into a small voltage.

Insulation

Keeps heat from leaking out, so the hot side stays as warm as possible.

Thermometer

Lets us track how warm the waste gets over time.

HOW THE MODEL RUNS

Load

Biodegradable waste goes into the insulated chamber.

Warm up

Microorganisms start breaking it down and the pile heats up.

Transfer

The metal element carries that heat to one face of the TEG.

Generate

The other face stays cooler, and the TEG produces a small voltage.

What makes it work

The bigger the temperature difference across the TEG, the more voltage it gives. That's why good insulation on the hot side and cooling on the other side matter.

Be honest about the limits

A small amount of waste makes a small amount of heat. Our model is a proof of concept that shows the principle, and it will not power your house.

CASE STUDY: KOLKATA & THE EAST KOLKATA WETLANDS

a city that already treats waste as a resource

Why Kolkata?

The East Kolkata Wetlands (EKW) are a Ramsar-listed wetland system on the city's eastern edge. For decades, local communities have used the city's sewage and organic waste to feed fish ponds and vegetable plots. It is one of the world's best-known examples of a living circular economy, and it sits right beside the Dhapa landfill, where much of the city's solid waste ends up.

EcoTherm fits this story: if organic waste is already a resource here, can it also be a small source of energy?

WHY IT'S DIFFERENT

Innovation

Combines organic-waste management with energy recovery. Waste isn't only something to dispose of. It's a heat source.

Cost-effective

The waste provides the heat, so no extra fuel is needed. The parts are inexpensive and easy to find.

Social impact

Encourages waste segregation and awareness about the circular economy in homes, schools, restaurants and housing societies.

QUESTIONS

How much electricity does it make?

Only a small amount. Our model is a demonstration of the principle, not a power source.

What's the Seebeck effect?

When two sides of a thermoelectric material are at different temperatures, a voltage appears across it.

How could it improve?

Better insulation, better heat exchangers and more effective cooling would widen the temperature difference and recover more energy.

Can I do this at home?

Start with the easy part: segregate your wet waste and compost it.
